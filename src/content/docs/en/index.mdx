<Section id="language-model" locale="en">

Give an LLM such as ChatGPT some text and a plausible continuation follows. What happens inside is simpler than it looks: <strong>it reads the text so far and gives every possible next word a probability</strong>.

Try it. What comes after “Yesterday I”? The probabilities below are what the small machine built in this article actually computes. Switch the first word to “Tomorrow”.

<StoryOpening client:load locale="en" />

Changing only the first word moves the favourite from “went” to “will”. The machine reads not just the last word but the words before it.

Appending the favourite makes the text one word longer, and the machine computes again from the longer text. After “Yesterday I went” the end marker is largest, so it stops there. When an LLM writes a long answer, it is running this loop hundreds or thousands of times.

This article builds that machine from scratch: words become numbers (chapter 2), the last position learns to read earlier words (chapters 3–4), probabilities become a sentence (chapter 5), learning sets the numbers (chapter 6), and we compare it with a real LLM (chapter 7). Where you might wonder about the calculation, a closed “Check the numbers” is waiting. The story carries on without opening it.

<Expand id="math-conditional" summary="How is “the next word’s probability” written in symbols?">

As P(went | Yesterday I) = 0.881. What follows the bar is the text so far; what precedes it is the candidate next word. The probabilities of the six candidates add up to 1.

</Expand>

<Note variant="limit" title="In a real LLM">
There are tens or hundreds of thousands of candidates rather than six, and the unit is a piece of a word (a token) rather than a word. A high probability means “this often comes next”, not that the content is true.
</Note>

<Section id="tokens" locale="en">

A machine cannot compute with letters. It can only multiply and add numbers, so the first step is to give every word some numbers.

Every word gets a number (an ID) and four values. The four places are named past, future, person and verb, and a word gets a 1 in each place whose property it has. A row of numbers like this is a <strong>vector</strong>, and each place in it a <strong>slot</strong>.

<WordTable locale="en" />

Now words can be compared as numbers. “went” and “will” both have a 1 in the verb slot and differ only in past and future. To tell them apart, the machine has to look at the time slots. That is where the next chapter starts.

<Expand id="math-shape" summary="Check the numbers: what are the ID and the number of slots?">

The ID is the word’s number. The machine uses it to find that word’s row in the table, which is called the embedding table. The number alone is not used because “Yesterday” being 0 and “Tomorrow” being 1 says nothing about both being about time.

The number of slots is the dimension. This machine’s vectors are four-dimensional. Stacking the vectors of two words gives a 2 × 4 table: 2 is the number of words, 4 the number of slots.

</Expand>

<Note variant="limit" title="In a real LLM">
This article’s slot names and values were designed to be readable. A real model has thousands of slots whose values are learned, so they rarely have simple names. It also has a vector per word piece (token) rather than per word; in this article one word is one token.
</Note>

<Section id="context" locale="en">

Time to build a first machine that chooses the next word. The simplest idea is to <strong>look only at the last word</strong>.

The machine reads the last word’s vector and gives every candidate a score. A 1 in the past slot raises the score of “went”, a 1 in the future slot raises the score of “will”, and so on. The table holding these rules we will call the <strong>next-word score table</strong>. The higher the score, the higher the probability.

Give it the two sentences.

<LastWordOnly locale="en" part="verdict" />

“Yesterday” and “Tomorrow” sit at the start of the sentence, but this machine only sees the last word, “I”. “I” carries no time information, so nothing separates “went” from “will”.
<strong>The last position needs a way to take information from earlier words</strong>. That is the next chapter’s attention.

<span id="similarity" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

<Expand id="math-softmax" summary="Check the numbers: how are the scores and probabilities computed?">

The score table says, for every candidate, how much each slot adds to its score. A positive number raises the score when the slot is present; a negative one lowers it. The “went” column, for example, gives +2 to the past slot and −2 to the future slot.

<ScoreTable locale="en" />

A score is the vector and the table’s column multiplied slot by slot and added up. This calculation is the <strong>dot product</strong>. The vector of “I” has a 1 only in the person slot, so the person row of the table becomes the scores.

<LastWordOnly locale="en" part="scores" />

<strong>Softmax</strong> turns scores into probabilities. It raises e to each score, which makes every value positive, then divides by the total. Looking only at “I”, the scores are Yesterday 0, Tomorrow 0, I −2, went 1, will 1, end 0. Since e<sup>1</sup> ≈ 2.718, e<sup>0</sup> = 1 and e<sup>−2</sup> ≈ 0.135, the total is about 8.57 and the probability of “went” is 2.718 ÷ 8.57 ≈ 0.317.

$$
\mathrm{softmax}(s)_i = \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}
$$

</Expand>

<Note variant="limit" title="In a real LLM">
The score table is learned too, not written by a person (chapter 6). Its candidates are the whole vocabulary, so it has thousands of rows and tens of thousands of columns or more.
</Note>

<Section id="attention" locale="en">

What should the position of “I” take from the earlier word? The next word is a verb such as “went” or “will”, and which one fits depends on <strong>time</strong>. So this position has to find time information in the earlier words and bring it in.

Attention does this the way you might look for a book in a library.

- The current position holds a <strong>question</strong>. At “I”, the question is “I need time information”.
- Every earlier word carries a <strong>label</strong>. The label of “Yesterday” says “I have time information”; the label of “I” says “I have person information”.
- The position takes more <strong>content</strong> from words whose labels match its question.

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="flow" />

It takes 89.3% from “Yesterday”, whose label matches the question, and 10.7% from itself. The past information taken this way is added to the original values, and the result goes back into chapter 3’s score table.

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="result" />

The score table did not change at all. The only difference is what the position of “I” took from the earlier word, and that alone puts “went” first after “Yesterday” and “will” first after “Tomorrow”.
This exchange of information between words of the same text is <strong>attention</strong>. Question, label and content are Query, Key and Value, written Q, K and V.

<Expand id="math-attention" summary="Check the numbers: where does 89.3% come from?">

<strong>Questions and labels as numbers.</strong> Questions and labels are vectors too, with two slots: time and person. They are made by multiplying a word’s vector by coefficients. In this example the coefficients are rules such as “a person word’s position asks for time with strength 3” and “the past and future slots become a time label”.

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="qk" />

<strong>Match scores.</strong> How well a question matches a label is measured by multiplying matching slots and adding (the dot product).

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="scores" />

<strong>Shares.</strong> The match scores are divided by the square root of the number of slots (2), then softmax turns them into shares that add up to 1. The division is there because more slots tend to give larger sums, which would push the shares too far to one side.

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="shares" />

<strong>Taking.</strong> Each word’s content is multiplied by its share and added. In this example a word’s content is simply its own vector.

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="take" />

<strong>Adding to the original values.</strong> Keeping only what was taken would shrink the position’s own person information to 0.107, so the taken values are added to the original vector. The new values hold both the person and the past, and they are what goes into the score table.

<StoryAttention locale="en" part="add" />

<strong>As one formula.</strong> With the coefficients that make questions, labels and content written W<sub>Q</sub>, W<sub>K</sub> and W<sub>V</sub>, and the number of slots written d<sub>k</sub>:

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

</Expand>

<span id="positions" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

### Only earlier words are read

The position of “I” does not read words that come after it. While writing, those words do not exist yet, and during learning (chapter 6) reading ahead would mean seeing the very answer it has to guess. So every position is masked to read only itself and earlier words. This is the <strong>causal mask</strong>.

<MaskTable locale="en" />

Attention also does not know where a word stands. So real models add different numbers for different places to the word vectors, called <strong>position information</strong>, to tell them the order. This article’s short example cannot mix up its order, so it leaves position information out.

<Note variant="limit" title="In a real LLM">
The coefficients for questions, labels and content are learned too, and they are not rules a person can read. Shares are computed from the current input and change when it changes; a large share alone does not show that a word caused the prediction. Position information is added as sine and cosine values in the original Transformer paper, while recent models rotate questions and labels according to their position, among other methods.
</Note>

<Section id="generation" locale="en">

The machine only computes probabilities. Choosing a word from them is a separate <strong>rule</strong>, and with the same probabilities different rules can produce different text.

The simplest rule is <strong>greedy</strong>: always take the favourite. The result is the same every time, but always picking the safest word tends to make text monotonous. <strong>Sampling</strong> draws at random in proportion to the probabilities. Here are both rules on the probabilities after “Yesterday I”.

<GenerationSteps locale="en" part="choose" />

How boldly to draw is set by the <strong>temperature</strong>. A low temperature concentrates the probability on the favourite; a high one spreads it out.

<GenerationSteps locale="en" part="temperature" />

<Expand id="math-temperature" summary="Check the numbers: where does the temperature enter the calculation?">

The scores are divided by the temperature T before softmax, so a probability becomes e<sup>s/T</sup> divided by the sum of all such terms. Below 1, T widens the gaps between scores and the favourite grows; above 1 it narrows them and the probabilities even out. At T = 1 they are the original probabilities.

</Expand>

Appending the chosen word lengthens the input, and the machine computes again from the longer input. It stops when the end marker comes out.

<GenerationSteps locale="en" part="loop" />

<Note variant="limit" title="In a real LLM">
To avoid recomputing everything each time, the labels and content of earlier words are stored and reused (the KV cache). There are also more choosing rules, such as drawing only from the few top candidates.
</Note>

<Section id="training" locale="en">

So far the score table and the coefficients were written by a person who thought up the rules. A real LLM has billions of coefficients, far too many to write by hand. They are set by <strong>learning</strong>.

The material for learning is plain text. Even the single sentence “Yesterday I went” yields three questions: take the beginning as input and the very next word as the answer. Thanks to chapter 4’s mask, all three are answered in one calculation.

<LearningViews locale="en" part="pairs" />

The table’s <strong>loss</strong> says how wrong the machine is: close to 0 when the answer gets a high probability, larger as that probability gets smaller. On the first question the machine gave “I” after “Yesterday” only 1.8%, so the loss is as large as 4.04. Learning nudges the coefficients so that losses like this go down.

Let us try it. A machine that has not learned yet ignores the time slots, so after “Yesterday I” it cannot tell “went” from “will”. The answer is “went”, so the (past, went) entry of the score table is changed once to raise its probability.

<LearningViews locale="en" part="compare" />

One step is enough to raise the probability of “went” from 37.9% to 50.0%. Repeating it over a great many sentences settles rules such as “after the past comes went” into the numbers. The values a person wrote into this article’s score table as rules are what a real model finds by itself this way.

<Expand id="math-gradient" summary="Check the numbers: which way, and by how much?">

If p is the probability given to the answer, the loss is −ln p. How much the loss changes when a coefficient changes a little is the <strong>gradient</strong>, and one step is taken in the direction opposite the gradient. The gradient for the (past, went) coefficient is negative, so making that coefficient larger lowers the loss.

This is the score table before learning: the past and future slots of the “went” and “will” columns are all 0.

<LearningViews locale="en" part="table" />

<LearningViews locale="en" part="gradient" />

Differentiating the loss −ln p with respect to each candidate’s score gives (that candidate’s probability − 1 if it is the answer, else 0). The score of “went” multiplies each slot of the new values by the “went” column and adds, so the gradient for the (past, went) coefficient is the past slot value × (the probability of “went” − 1).

</Expand>

<Note variant="limit" title="In a real LLM">
Every coefficient is updated together, not only the score table: questions, labels and content as well. Passing the gradient back from the last layer to the first is called backpropagation. This repeats over trillions of tokens.
</Note>

<Section id="real-llm" locale="en">

This article’s machine and a real LLM choose the next word on the same principle. They differ in size, and in who set the numbers.

<span id="blocks" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

A real model uses this machine’s parts at a larger size and in many layers. In the picture below, the blue boxes are the parts this article’s machine also has.

<BlockStack locale="en" />

- It keeps <strong>several questions</strong>. The position of “I” only looked for time, but real text also needs who, where and more. One set of question, label and content is called a head.
- A <strong>per-word computation</strong> (the FFN) combines slots into new properties. Attention brings information over from other words, but it never combines the slots within one word.
- As in chapter 4, results are <strong>added to the original values</strong>, and the numbers are <strong>kept in range</strong> so that repeated additions do not let them grow out of hand.
- This bundle is a <strong>block</strong>, and it is <strong>stacked dozens of times</strong>. A higher block reads values a lower block has already mixed, so it can handle more complex relations.

| | This article’s machine | A real LLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Candidates | 6 words | tens to hundreds of thousands of tokens |
| Vector slots | 4, named by a person | thousands, learned and rarely nameable |
| Attention | 1 question | dozens of heads per block |
| Blocks | 1, without FFN or range keeping | dozens |
| Coefficients | dozens, written as rules by a person | billions or more, learned |
| Training data | 1 sentence | trillions of tokens |

Whatever the size, the work is the same: turn the text so far into vectors, let each position take what it needs from earlier words, pass through the blocks to the next word’s probabilities, then choose one and append it.

One last point to keep in mind. The probabilities an LLM gives are close to <strong>what tends to come next</strong> in the text it learned from. A plausible sentence and a true one are not the same thing, so a high probability does not guarantee accurate content.

This machine’s design comes from the Transformer in the 2017 paper *Attention Is All You Need*. That paper built a translator, so it has a part that reads the source (the encoder) and a part that writes the translation (the decoder). Most of today’s LLMs are greatly enlarged versions of the writing part.
