<p data-model-intro>An LLM computes <strong>next-token probabilities</strong> from its input. Append one choice, then recompute with the longer input.</p>

<Expand id="lesson-model-scope" summary="About this small model">

This illustrative model computes from two inputs and fixed coefficients. It omits position calculations and only references earlier positions and the current one. Real LLMs learn coefficients by reducing prediction errors over many texts.
Tokens are chunks of text: whole words or word parts. The end marker is a stopping token. A high probability does not guarantee factual content.

</Expand>

<Expand id="math-conditional" summary="Which context does this probability describe?">

In P(end \| I went), the text after the bar is the available input; the token before it is a next candidate. A changed input can produce changed probabilities with the same coefficients.

</Expand>

<Section id="attention" locale="en" doc="walkthrough">
<span id="name-the-roles-we-have-already-used-q--k--v" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

<div data-main-lesson>

The vector for the final position, <strong>went</strong>, is (0, 1, 0, 1): it holds information about that chunk alone. Yet what comes next depends on the earlier context.
After “I went” a destination such as “to school” fits; after “it went” something like “wrong” might. So the final position <strong>takes content from the positions it can read and mixes it into its own values</strong>. That calculation is <strong>attention</strong>.

<AttentionFlow locale="en" />

The shares and new values come from three steps: ① <strong>score</strong> how well each position matches what the current one is looking for, ② turn the scores into <strong>shares</strong> that add up to 1, and ③ <strong>add</strong> each position’s content in proportion to its share.
This example’s coefficients were chosen so the arithmetic can be followed by hand; they have not learned anything like the “I went / it went” difference. Only the method is followed here.

<span id="numbers" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

### ① Scores: make comparison values, then multiply matching entries

Comparing needs comparison values first. Multiply each of went’s four input values by a fixed coefficient and add them to make one new value. Repeating this for every output is <strong>matrix multiplication</strong>; here it makes two values.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="projection" />

<div id="similarity">

Now match the current comparison values <strong>(0, 2)</strong> against each position’s values. Simply adding all four numbers of the two rows gives 4 for I and 4 for went, which tells them apart not at all.
<strong>Multiplying matching entries and adding</strong> gives 0×0 + 2×2 = 4 for I and 0×2 + 2×0 = 0 for went. The position whose values sit in the same places as the current ones scores higher.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="comparison" />

To compare against several positions at once, stand each reference row up as a column and place the columns side by side. One current row times that table then gives every score.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="transpose" />
<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="scores" />

</div>

<Expand id="math-dot" summary="How is 0×0 + 2×2 written in symbols?">

The dot product of q = (0, 2) and k = (0, 2) is written q · k = 0×0 + 2×2 = 4. With the reference rows stood up as the columns of Kᵀ, the single product qKᵀ = (4, 0) gives both scores at once.
Every entry of a matrix product is one such dot product.

</Expand>

<span id="proportion" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

### ② Shares: turn the scores into proportions that add up to 1

Scores can be negative and their total is arbitrary. To serve as shares of content, they must all be at least 0 and add up to 1.
<strong>Softmax</strong> makes each score positive with the exponential function, then divides by the total. The exponential keeps the order of the scores and widens the gap between large and small ones.
First, though, the scores are divided by √2. The more values a comparison uses, the larger a sum of products tends to be, so dividing by the square root of that count (2 here) keeps the spread in check.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="shares" />

<Expand id="math-softmax" summary="Where does 94.4% come from?">

The scores divided by √2 are (2.828, 0). Since e<sup>2.828</sup> ≈ 16.92 and e<sup>0</sup> = 1, the share for I is 16.92 ÷ (16.92 + 1) ≈ 0.944 and the share for went is 1 ÷ 17.92 ≈ 0.056.

$$
\mathrm{softmax}(s)_i = \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}
$$

</Expand>

### ③ Gathering: add the content in proportion to the shares

The content to take is also made from the same input, with yet another coefficient table. Multiplying I’s content (3, 0) by <strong>94.4%</strong> and went’s content (0, 3) by <strong>5.6%</strong>, then adding, gives the new values for the went position: (2.833, 0.167).
Multiplying by shares and adding like this is a <strong>weighted sum</strong>.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="aggregation" />

### The three roles have names: Q, K and V

So far the same input has produced three sets of numbers. The current position’s comparison values are the <strong>Query (Q)</strong>, each position’s comparison values are its <strong>Key (K)</strong>, and the content to take is its <strong>Value (V)</strong>. The coefficient tables that make them are written W_Q, W_K and W_V.
Positions within one sequence mix with each other, hence <strong>self-attention</strong>. The new values still belong to the went position.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="attention" />

<Expand id="math-attention" summary="How do the three steps become one formula?">

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

In this example the last row of QKᵀ is (4, 0), d<sub>k</sub> = 2, the shares after softmax are (0.944, 0.056), and multiplying by V gives (2.833, 0.167).

</Expand>

<Note variant="limit" title="Shares are computed from this input">
W_Q, W_K and W_V are coefficients set by learning, but shares such as 94.4% and 5.6% are computed from this particular input. A different input gives different shares with the same coefficients.
A large share only means that position’s content was mixed in heavily; on its own it does not show that position caused the prediction.
</Note>

</div>

<Section id="blocks" locale="en" doc="walkthrough">

<div data-main-lesson>

### Preserve the original input by adding it

Passing attention output alone may lose information from the input. Adding matching input entries provides a <strong>residual connection</strong>.
Then layer normalization adjusts the scale of values within this token.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="residual" />

### Transform the normalized values within this token

Attention has already gathered information between tokens. The <strong>FFN</strong> now widens and narrows the values of each token separately.
Its input is exactly the preceding normalization output. ReLU replaces negative entries with zero.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="feed-forward" />

### Add and normalize again

Add the row entering the FFN to the row leaving it, then normalize again. This produces <strong>the final representation at went</strong>, ready to score next candidates.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="hidden" />

</div>

<Section id="big-picture" locale="en" doc="walkthrough">
<span id="use-the-blocks-final-representation-to-compare-vocabulary-candidates" class="lesson-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span>

<div data-main-lesson>

Receive the final representation from the preceding unit unchanged. Multiplying by vocabulary coefficients gives scores for <strong>I / went / to school / end</strong>.
Softmax converts those scores into probabilities for the next chunk.

<LessonComputation locale="en" stage="readout" />

<strong>To school has the largest probability, 71.8%.</strong> The input still contains only I / went; to school is a next candidate.
Training compares this prediction with a target. Generation chooses a chunk from these probabilities and appends it.

<Expand id="math-readout" summary="How do input-position shares differ from vocabulary probabilities?">

The two attention shares retrieve content from the two given positions. These four probabilities refer to four next-vocabulary candidates. The vocabulary coefficients turn the final representation into candidate scores.

</Expand>

</div>
